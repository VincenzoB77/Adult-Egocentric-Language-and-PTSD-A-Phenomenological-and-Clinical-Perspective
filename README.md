Abstract

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) has traditionally been approached through cognitive, behavioral, neurobiological, and psychodynamic frameworks. While these approaches have significantly contributed to understanding traumatic suffering, the role of language as a constitutive structure of subjective experience remains insufficiently explored. Drawing from the analytical phenomenological psychology developed by Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo, this article argues that adult egocentric language represents a fundamental diagnostic and therapeutic dimension in PTSD. Egocentric language is not merely verbal behavior but a manifestation of the individual's relationship with selfhood, temporality, embodiment, and interpersonal existence. Through a phenomenological analysis of trauma narratives, the study examines how linguistic self-reference reflects the fragmentation of experience characteristic of post-traumatic conditions. The article proposes that alterations in the use of first-person language reveal disruptions in identity continuity and meaning-making processes. Furthermore, it explores how the therapeutic reconstruction of egocentric language may facilitate psychological integration, resilience, and post-traumatic growth.

Keywords: PTSD, trauma, egocentric language, phenomenology, analytical phenomenological psychology, selfhood, identity, Bumbalo.

Introduction

The experience of psychological trauma presents one of the greatest challenges to human consciousness. Trauma is not simply an event that occurs in time; it becomes an organizing principle that transforms the way individuals perceive themselves, others, and reality. In PTSD, the traumatic experience remains active within consciousness, continuously influencing emotional, cognitive, and interpersonal functioning.

Most contemporary models describe trauma through neurobiological dysregulation, distorted cognitions, maladaptive emotional responses, or unconscious conflicts. While each of these perspectives contributes valuable insights, they often overlook a crucial dimension: the language through which traumatic experience is lived and narrated.

Language is not merely a tool used to describe psychological suffering. It actively shapes suffering itself. Every traumatic memory is encoded, maintained, interpreted, and communicated through linguistic processes. Consequently, understanding PTSD requires examining how language reflects and structures subjective experience.

Within the theoretical framework developed by Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo, particular attention is given to egocentric language. This concept refers to the linguistic expressions through which individuals position themselves as subjects of experience. Such language includes explicit self-references, first-person narratives, personal evaluations, self-descriptions, and subjective interpretations of reality.

From this perspective, egocentric language becomes a privileged observatory of psychic functioning. The manner in which traumatized individuals speak about themselves may reveal the degree of fragmentation, alienation, or integration characterizing their post-traumatic adaptation.

The central thesis of this article is that disturbances in adult egocentric language constitute a phenomenological marker of PTSD and that restoring coherent self-referential discourse may represent a significant therapeutic objective.

Trauma and the Disruption of the Self

Phenomenological psychology begins with lived experience rather than diagnostic categories. Before PTSD becomes a diagnosis, it is experienced as a radical disruption of existence.

Traumatic events challenge the individual's assumptions regarding safety, predictability, continuity, and personal agency. The world that once appeared familiar becomes threatening and unpredictable. Simultaneously, the self may appear unfamiliar, fragmented, or disconnected. Trauma therefore affects not only memory but also identity.

Classical phenomenologists such as Edmund Husserl, Martin Heidegger, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, and Ludwig Binswanger emphasized that human beings experience themselves as temporal and embodied subjects. Personal identity emerges through a continuous narrative process linking past, present, and future within a meaningful whole. PTSD disrupts this continuity.

Patients frequently report experiences such as:

• "I am not the same person anymore."

• "Part of me died that day."

• "I don't recognize myself."

• "I feel detached from who I used to be."

These expressions illustrate more than emotional suffering. They reveal a crisis of self-experience. According to Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's framework, such disturbances become visible through transformations in egocentric language. The traumatized person no longer speaks from a stable center of subjectivity. Instead, language reflects fragmentation, estrangement, and existential uncertainty.

Egocentric Language as a Phenomenological Construct

Traditional developmental psychology associates egocentric language primarily with childhood, particularly through the works of Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky. However, Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo extends the concept beyond childhood development, proposing that egocentric language remains a permanent feature of adult psychological functioning.

In adulthood, egocentric language serves several key functions:

• Construction of self-identity.

• Organization of subjective experience.

• Emotional regulation.

• Meaning attribution.

• Integration of autobiographical memory.

Every statement beginning with "I" reflects a positioning of consciousness toward reality. Examples include:

• "I feel vulnerable."

• "I cannot trust anyone."

• "I survived."

• "I am responsible."

• "I am broken."

From a phenomenological viewpoint, these statements do not merely describe experiences. They actively constitute experience. Consequently, changes in egocentric language indicate transformations in the structure of selfhood. In PTSD, linguistic self-reference often becomes constrained, distorted, or fragmented, revealing deeper alterations in the individual’s experiential world.

Linguistic Markers of PTSD and Neuro-Phenomenological Analysis

The analysis of trauma narratives consistently reveals specific linguistic patterns linked to profound neurobiological and experiential disruptions. Below are detailed clinical vignettes demonstrating these markers under the framework of the Bumbalo Model.

1. Fragmented Self-Reference, Temporal Collapse, and Limbic Hyperactivation

Patients often alternate between first-person and impersonal language while remaining linguistically frozen in the present tense, reflecting an active neurobiological hijack.

[Clinical Vignette 1] Clinical Vignette Context:

A 34-year-old combat veteran diagnosed with severe PTSD. In this session, the patient is describing a sensory trigger (a loud exhaust backfire) that occurred in a parking lot earlier that week.

The Fragmented/Frozen State:

"I am walking to my car, and suddenly the sound hits. The blast happens. I'm back in the vehicle, looking at the smoke. My heart is pounding out of my chest, my vision narrows, and you don't feel your body anymore. It just goes numb. The hands are shaking on the steering wheel, but it feels like someone else’s hands. You see the fire, and you know there’s no way out."

Analysis: Neuro-Phenomenological Analysis (Bumbalo Model):

The narrative demonstrates an immediate temporal collapse driven by an acute limbic hyperactivation. The auditory trigger bypasses cortical processing, causing the amygdala to signal an immediate existential threat. This neurobiological hijack floods the system with stress hormones, manifesting physiologically as tachycardia and visual narrowing. To survive this overwhelming autonomic surge, the nervous system deploys a dissociative defense: a severe pronominal shift occurs. The patient abandons the first-person "I" and moves to the impersonal "you" and localized somatic descriptions ("the hands", "the body"). This linguistic shift is the exact symbolic marker of Broca's area inhibition and a failure of hippocampal contextualization—the brain cannot register the event as "past," forcing the consciousness to relive the trauma as an immediate, unmediated living present.

The Integrated/Restructured State (Later Phase of Therapy):

"When I heard that sound in the parking lot last week, I felt a sudden wave of panic and my heart started racing—my amygdala was clearly misinterpreting the noise as danger. But instead of dissociating, I used my breath to calm my nervous system. I looked around, engaged my prefrontal cortex, and I realized that I was safe in the present moment. It was a bottom-up alarm, but I knew it was just a memory, not happening now."

2. Reduction of Agency, Negative Identity Narratives, and Prefrontal Deactivation

Adult egocentric language in PTSD frequently reveals diminished personal agency and a rigid transition from behavioral evaluation to permanent existential identity.

[Clinical Vignette 2] Clinical Vignette Context:

A 29-year-old survivor of prolonged interpersonal domestic abuse. The patient is reflecting on her current inability to make career decisions or establish boundaries with family members.

The Fragmented/Frozen State:

"Opportunities come up, but things just happen to me, I don’t choose them. My brain just goes completely blank when I have to decide. My life was completely destroyed back then. When you are broken like this, you can’t build anything real. I am just a damaged person now. The trauma dictates the rules, and my nervous system just follows them passively."

Analysis: Neuro-Phenomenological Analysis (Bumbalo Model):

This discourse highlights a profound reduction of personal agency paired with prefrontal deactivation. Chronic relational trauma alters the executive functioning networks; when faced with choices, the patient experiences a "blankness" that reflects a localized neurobiological shutdown of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), hindering top-down emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility. Phenomenologically, this neurobiological paralysis solidifies into a negative identity narrative. The patient does not say "I feel damaged by what happened," but rather states "I am just a damaged person now." This grammatical transition transforms a neurobiological survival response (tonic immobility and cognitive freezing) into a rigid, non-negotiable existential identity, locking the self out of any future-oriented possibilities.

The Integrated/Restructured State (Later Phase of Therapy):

"The abuse I went through kept my brain in a constant survival mode, which damaged my self-esteem and made decision-making feel terrifying. But I now understand that my passivity was just a neurobiological defense mechanism, not who I am. I survived that environment. Now, I am actively retraining my brain to make choices, step by step, and I am the one reclaiming the executive control over my own future."

3. Embodied Experience, Metaphorical Fixation, and Polyvagal Dorsal Shutdown

Trauma is carried within bodily experience. In severe states, the uncoupling of social engagement drives somatic immobilization expressed via fixed spatial metaphors.

[Clinical Vignette 3] Clinical Vignette Context:

A 42-year-old patient who survived a near-fatal motor vehicle accident, presenting with severe hypervigilance, somatic tension, and chronic emotional numbing.

The Fragmented/Frozen State:

"I live my life behind a thick brick wall. I can see people on the other side, but I can’t reach them. When I try to speak about the accident, my throat closes up, my voice drops, and I feel totally hollow inside. I feel frozen, totally heavy, like my legs are trapped in concrete. It’s always there, blocking everything."

Analysis: Neuro-Phenomenological Analysis (Bumbalo Model):

Here, the lived body (Leib) expresses the trauma through restrictive somatic and spatial metaphors that directly map onto a Polyvagal Dorsal Vagal Shutdown. The visceral descriptions—the closing of the throat, the loss of vocal prosody, and the sensation of being "trapped in concrete"—are linguistic encodings of a primitive phylogenetic defense system. When the sympathetic nervous system is overwhelmed, the dorsal vagal pathway takes over, dropping heart rate, numbing pain, and inducing behavioral immobility (freezing). The phrase "behind a thick brick wall" is not mere poetic license; it represents the actual neurobiological architecture of the patient's subjective reality, where the uncoupling of the Social Engagement System cuts off the intersubjective capacity to connect with others, keeping the body neuroceptively trapped in danger.

The Integrated/Restructured State (Later Phase of Therapy):

"For a long time, my nervous system was stuck in a dorsal vagal drop—I felt completely disconnected, numb, and frozen inside my own body. But now, when I feel that tightness in my throat or that heaviness in my legs, I recognize it as my body's old way of trying to protect me from impact. I can anchor myself in safety, activate my social engagement system, and speak my truth without letting that physiological defense build a wall between me and the people I love."

Dissociation and the Loss of Linguistic Ownership

One of the most significant phenomena associated with severe PTSD is dissociation, involving disruptions in memory, perception, identity, and consciousness. From Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's perspective, dissociation manifests linguistically through weakened self-reference.

Individuals may describe traumatic events using detached or impersonal expressions: "The body reacted," "That person was me, but not really me," or "It happened." Notice the absence of a stable "I." The grammatical structure itself reflects a crisis of ownership.

Phenomenological psychology interprets this pattern as a disruption in the intentional relation between subject and experience. The traumatic experience exists, but the subject cannot fully claim it as part of personal history. Therapeutic work therefore requires facilitating the re-establishment of first-person ownership without overwhelming emotional exposure.

Egocentric Language as the Architecture of Subjective Reality

One of the most original implications of Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's analytical phenomenological psychology is the proposition that language does not merely express psychic reality but actively participates in its construction. This view transcends the traditional understanding of language as a communicative instrument and positions linguistic activity as one of the fundamental structures through which consciousness organizes experience.

Within PTSD, traumatic events frequently exceed the individual's preexisting symbolic capacities. The trauma introduces experiences that appear incompatible with previous systems of meaning. Consequently, the subject struggles to integrate the event into a coherent personal narrative. This difficulty manifests linguistically through repetitions, interruptions, contradictions, emotional discontinuities, and sudden shifts in perspective. These linguistic alterations reveal the underlying fragmentation of the experiential field itself.

According to Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's perspective, the analysis of linguistic structures permits access to the dynamic organization of conscious life. The manner in which the subject uses pronouns, tenses, metaphors, descriptive categories, and affective labels provides essential information about the current state of psychological integration. The traumatized subject frequently oscillates between excessive identification with the traumatic event and complete avoidance of traumatic content. Between these two extremes, language loses its integrative function.

In clinical settings, one commonly observes patients whose narratives appear trapped within a cyclical pattern of suffering. The same sentences are repeated across multiple sessions: "I can't move on," "I cannot forget," "It is always there," or "My life stopped that day." The persistence of these expressions reveals that language has become organized around traumatic fixation. The event no longer occupies a place within the biography; rather, the biography becomes organized around the event. Phenomenologically, this represents a collapse of existential flexibility.

The Crisis of Temporality in PTSD

A central concept within phenomenology is temporality. Human existence unfolds through the integration of past experiences, present awareness, and future expectations. Psychological health depends largely upon the fluid interaction among these temporal dimensions. Trauma profoundly disrupts this organization.

Many individuals suffering from PTSD report a strange sensation that the traumatic event never truly ended. Nightmares, flashbacks, intrusive memories, and physiological reactions all contribute to this phenomenon. However, Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's approach emphasizes that alterations in temporality become especially visible through egocentric language, as shown in the present-tense constructs highlighted in our clinical cases.

This temporal collapse severely affects personal identity. Since identity develops through narrative continuity, disruptions in temporal organization inevitably influence self-perception. In many cases, future-oriented language becomes significantly reduced. Statements such as "I don't know what will happen," "I cannot imagine my future," or "I have no plans" illustrate a contraction of existential horizons. The person ceases to speak from the position of someone who has possibilities. Instead, language reveals confinement within the traumatic present. The restoration of healthy temporality therefore represents one of the primary objectives of psychotherapy.

Shame, Guilt, and the Transformation of Self-Reference

Among the emotional experiences associated with PTSD, shame and guilt occupy a particularly significant role. Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's theoretical framework suggests that these emotions become embedded within egocentric language and gradually transform personal identity.

Consider the crucial difference between the following statements: "I made a mistake" (which refers to behavior) and "I am a mistake" (which refers to permanent identity). Traumatic experiences frequently produce this transition. Victims of abuse, violence, war, or disasters often internalize distorted self-perceptions that become integrated into their linguistic self-definition, such as "I am weak," "I am dirty," "I am guilty," or "I am worthless."

Over time, such linguistic constructions become self-reinforcing. Phenomenological psychotherapy seeks to interrupt this process by helping individuals distinguish between experience and identity. Instead of identifying completely with traumatic meanings, patients learn to relate to their experiences from a reflective position, marking a crucial turning point in recovery.

Intersubjectivity and the Social Dimension of Trauma

Human beings exist within networks of interpersonal relationships; consequently, trauma is never purely individual. Phenomenological psychology describes this dimension through the concept of intersubjectivity. Every experience of self emerges within relationships with others.

According to Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's approach, egocentric language should be understood not merely as self-referential speech but as speech situated within interpersonal contexts. Trauma frequently damages trust, altering language through expressions like "No one understands me," "I cannot trust anybody," or "I am alone." The interpersonal environment loses its protective and validating functions. In therapeutic settings, the clinician plays an essential role in restoring intersubjective trust. Through empathic dialogue, expressions of despair may slowly evolve into expressions of openness, curiosity, and hope.

Clinical Applications of Bumbalo's Theory

The practical usefulness of the Bumbalo model becomes particularly evident within psychotherapy. Clinicians focus systematically on linguistic processes emerging during therapeutic dialogue across several key dimensions:

• Analysis of Pronoun Use: Tracking shifts between first-person, second-person, or impersonal constructions to measure emotional engagement or dissociation.

• Analysis of Agency: Observing whether the patient speaks as an active subject or passive object to predict psychological flexibility.

• Analysis of Metaphors: Unlocking immediate access to visceral and spatial architectures of suffering that evade literal vocabulary.

• Analysis of Temporal Language: Monitoring the transition from immediate present-tense reliving to integrated past-tense recollection.

Post-Traumatic Growth as a Reconstruction of the Self

Within the Bumbalo framework, post-traumatic growth is understood as the reconstruction of egocentric language at a higher level of existential complexity. As healing progresses, language moves away from pure fixation on loss to actively include discovery, with statements like "I learned," "I developed," "I changed," or "I found meaning." The trauma is integrated as a chapter within a comprehensive life story rather than a total definition of the self.

Therapeutic Implications and Goals

• Restoration of Narrative Continuity: Transforming the question from "What happened to me?" to "How has this experience become part of who I am?"

• Recovery of Agency: Shifting language deliberately to promote empowerment (e.g., from "I was destroyed" to "I survived").

• Emotional Symbolization: Converting sensory, neurobiological, and bodily fragments into meaningful, thinkable verbal content.

• Development of Reflective Self-Awareness: Distinguishing between the experiencing self and the event (e.g., "I experienced trauma" vs. "I am trauma").

Conclusion

The present article has explored the importance of adult egocentric language in Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder through the analytical phenomenological perspective inspired by the work of Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo. The central argument has been that traumatic suffering cannot be adequately understood solely through symptom-based descriptions, neurobiological mechanisms, or cognitive distortions. Rather, trauma must also be examined as a transformation of subjective experience, expressed and structured through language.

Within this framework, egocentric language emerges as a privileged access point to the lived world of the traumatized individual. The use of first-person expressions, personal narratives, self-definitions, temporal references, and embodied descriptions reveals how trauma reorganizes the individual's relationship with selfhood, corporeality, temporality, and interpersonal existence. Alterations in linguistic self-reference are therefore indicators of deeper existential, neurobiological, and psychological transformations.

Particular attention has been given to the original contribution of Dr. Vincenzo Bumbalo's analytical phenomenological psychology, which conceives language not simply as an instrument for describing reality but as one of the fundamental structures through which reality is subjectively constituted. From this perspective, therapeutic work on language becomes therapeutic work on consciousness itself. Ultimately, it suggests that the path to recovery is not solely the reduction of symptoms but the reconstruction of a coherent, meaningful, and authentically lived sense of self.
